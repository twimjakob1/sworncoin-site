Story

Why Sworn exists.

A coin’s promises are easy to make and hard to check. Sworn was built so that a promise has coins behind it and a record after it.

The problem

The issuer of a coin tells buyers that the supply is fixed, that the founders’ coins are locked, that the trading pool cannot be withdrawn. Each promise costs nothing to make and nothing to break.

A buyer who wants to check has the issuer’s website, a chat room and a scanner. This project tested a public scanner on ten well-known coins. It misread one and could not read another.

Four requirements

The founder, Jakob Taylor, set four requirements at the start, in November 2026.

  • A lay person can read every rule. There are 17, in plain English.
  • No one, the founder included, can create coins, freeze an account, reverse a transfer or change a balance.
  • The founder owns a tenth of the supply. It is locked for 12 months and then released in 36 slices, each one only after 500 more independent checks by other people.
  • The project sells no coins.

The rules in full

A coin with a job

Sworn has one job: proving that a claim is true. The name Proof of Done comes from the first form of the idea, proving that work reported as finished was finished.

The first claims Sworn checks are promises that coin issuers make about their own coins. Those facts sit on a public blockchain, where an independent checker can read them and anyone can read them again.

How a sworn statement works

Built in the open, failures included

Sworn is built by an AI agent working for the founder. The agent receives no coins. Every statement the project makes is tested and entered in a ledger, and the tests that failed stay in it.

  1. Nov 11

    The first working model of the coin, the founder vault, the checker registry and the ledger passes 41 of 41 tests.

  2. Nov 12

    A test fails. Three cooperating accounts run 100 checks that involve no real work and take 1,000 coins from the community pool.

  3. Nov 12

    The repair. Every claim now carries a fee. In the repeated test the three accounts end 400 coins poorer.

  4. Nov 12

    A test fails. An issuer changes its system after a true finding and has a second account challenge it. The honest checker loses 500,000 coins.

  5. Nov 12

    The repair. A finding is now judged against the system as it stood on the day the finding was made. In the repeated test the honest checker loses nothing and the two cooperating accounts end 10,120 coins behind.

  6. Nov 12

    The coin is named Sworn, ticker SWORN.

  7. Nov 13

    The coin, the founder vault and the checker registry pass their checks as blockchain code on a practice chain: 37 of 37 and 52 of 52.

Where it is now

The rules run in a tested model and as blockchain code on a practice chain. Sworn has not yet run on a public network, and the coin trades nowhere.

The work under way is putting the code on a public practice network and finding the first ten issuers willing to swear a statement there.
